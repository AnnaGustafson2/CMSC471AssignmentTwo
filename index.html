White Hat Visualization: Life Expectancy by Region and Sex

Our dataset comes from the World Bank Gender Statistics database and includes information about life expectancy for men and women across different regions from 1960 to 2019. We filtered the data to use the 7 World Bank regional aggregates (selected by country code) to compare life expectancy between genders and see how these differences have changed over time. We removed any rows with missing female/male values or years outside the range and then converted remaining values to numbers.

For our white-hat visualization, we wanted to show the data in a clear and accurate way. We chose a grouped bar chart that places women's and men's life expectancies next to each other for each region. The bar lengths encode the life expectancy values, making it easy to compare the differences between genders and regions. We used purple for women and teal for men to make the bars easy to tell apart.

One important design choice was starting the y-axis at zero. We chose to have the y-axis go from 0-90 in order to maintain that the bars are proportional to life expectancy. This prevents small differences from looking much larger than they actually are. We also implemented a data transformation to calculate life expectancy gaps (female life expectancy - male). This was used to add life expectancy gap labels above each pair of bars to show the exact difference in years between men and women. Smaller differences between bars may be hard to perceive in a bar chart, so we wanted to make those differences clear with the labels.

We included a sorting dropdown and a year slider so users can explore how life expectancy has changed over time. Users could clearly choose how to explore the data trends and sort by region, life expectancy, and largest gaps. We implemented a data transformation to calculate the average life expectancy for a region, which was used for the sorting functionality. We also added clear labels, a legend, and information about our data source to help users understand the chart. The section explaining our data source highlights how these gaps in life expectancies can reflect a number of behavioral, occupational, or health related factors. The bars have tooltips, providing more specific details and contextualizing each mark within the rest of the chart. We also implemented data transformations to calculate the gap change since 1960 and rank the regions by life expectancy gaps within each year, which were included in the tooltips for further insight.

Overall, our goal was to let users explore the data and come to their own conclusions without pushing a certain message.

Black Hat Visualization: Men's Life Expectancy Deficit Is Widening

Our black-hat visualization uses the same dataset as the white-hat version, but we made design choices that intentionally make the differences between men and women look larger than they actually are.

The main technique we used was changing the y-axis. Instead of starting at zero, we dynamically start it closer to the actual life expectancy values, dramatizing any differences in life expectancy. We shifted and set the y-axis to start from the year’s smallest male value (minus 5 and then rounded down to a multiple of 5) and end from the largest female value rounded up. We also removed the intuitive life expectancy gap label above the bars and opted not to include any extra details in the tooltips. This removes users’ understanding of crucial data details and makes the bars look much more different in height, even when the actual difference is only a few years. As a result, women's life expectancy appears much higher than men's.

We also used bright pink for women and orange for men against a dark background. These colors make the chart look more dramatic and draw more attention to the differences.

Another technique was how we presented the information. Our title, "Men's Life Expectancy Deficit Is Widening," pushes a narrative to viewers and makes it seem like men are falling further behind and need more attention. We implemented another data transformation that would calculate each region’s gap as a percentage change from its 1960 gap. We added a callout with a large percentage showing how much the gap has grown in MENA, which is the region with the largest percent gap increase since 1960. This makes the difference seem more serious, even though the actual change in years is small. Choosing to display the percentage only helps make the changes seem more drastic.

We also showed fewer regions than in the white-hat visualization. By cherry-picking from the dataset to only display regions with large enough life expectancy gap increases (>= 1 year) in the given time frame of 1960 to 2019, we could focus on the data that best supports our message.

Below our visualization, we also opted to include the bare minimum information about the data with no disclaimers about the possibility of other factors influencing these trends. This helps us force viewers to focus more on the narrative that we construct around the visualization by providing no other information other than what we want to show.

Overall, our black-hat visualization shows how changing the scale, colors, title, and data shown can make the same dataset tell a very different story. We aimed to manipulate the encodings (shifting axis, colors), data transformations (cherry-picking regions, using percent change), and titles and labels (headline, callouts, missing gap labels) in order to provide a skewed understanding of the data.

Development Process

Our team started by finding a dataset that we could use to create both an accurate and a misleading visualization. We chose life expectancy because we thought it would be interesting to see how different design choices could change the way people understand the same data.

We first planned out the white-hat visualization by choosing a grouped bar chart, colors, and interactive features like sorting and a year slider. Then, we planned the black-hat visualization by changing the y-axis, colors, and title to make the differences look more dramatic.

After planning both charts, we used Figma Make to turn our ideas into a UI/UX prototype. This helped us see what the webpage would look like before we started coding. We then used the prototype as a guide to develop the actual webpage and add the charts and interactive features.

Before coding the visualizations, we implemented a few transformations to extend the data and help us add new perspectives and information to the visualizations. Then, we implemented the visualizations and year slider feature, inspired by our bar chart and interactive visualization labs.

The parts that took the most time were organizing the data, making the charts interactive, and adjusting the design to match our prototype.

Extra Credit Feature

Using Figma Make, we prototyped a play button for the year slider towards the bottom of the screen. Through prompting, it added a button that would automatically move through the years and update both bar charts accordingly. This would allow users to watch how the life expectancies changed over time without having to manually move the slider themselves. This feature goes above and beyond to let us compare the ways in which the white-hat and black-hat visualization present the data and see how the black hat visualization manipulates our understanding of trends.

AI Usage

We used AI tools to help with the UI/UX design of our webpage. We mainly used Figma Make to create a prototype based on our ideas.

We gave Figma Make prompts explaining how we wanted the webpage to look, including the two bar charts, year slider, colors, and different y-axis scales. We adjusted our prompts to improve the design and make it closer to what we wanted.

Using AI helped us save time during the design process because we could quickly see different layouts and make changes before coding. It also helped provide the basic CSS styling that we could adapt to our implementation of the visualization to speed up the code process.
